## Scientific corrections and model boundaries

1. **Valence electrons, valency, ion charge and oxidation number are different concepts.** Main-group outer-shell examples are qualified; transition metals need more than outer-shell counting. FeCl2/FeCl3 illustrate variable valency. The book's historical “latent valency” subtraction is not presented as physical unused bond sites.
2. Formula derivation balances opposite charges, reduces the ratio and preserves parentheses around repeated polyatomic ions. Ionic crystals use **formula units**, not automatically independent NaCl/MgO molecules. NH4+/SO4^2− etc. have covalent connections within the ion and ionic attraction between ions; the two levels are not collapsed into one bond label.
3. Standard duet = **two electrons**, especially H/He's first shell, not any arbitrary even number of electrons. Octet is a limited main-group counting rule, not the only cause or universal prerequisite of bonding. BF3 has6 around B; the stated **isolated gas-phase** BeCl2 Lewis model has4 around Be, not a claim about polymeric solid BeCl2.
4. Bonding is explained by total-energy/attraction–repulsion considerations, not atoms consciously wanting electrons. Isolated gas-phase electron removal requires ionization energy. Simple transfer diagrams are bookkeeping, not proof that every isolated step releases energy.
5. Electron transfer forms ions; **opposite-charge electrostatic attraction is the ionic bond**. Lattices are extended. Proton number remains fixed on ordinary ion formation. NaH contains H−, so H is not +1 in every compound.
6. Each shared pair has two electrons. Local octet/duet counting can include the pair at both centres, but total distinct electrons must not be doubled. Lewis graphs show connectivity/pair accounting, not all 3D angles or O2 paramagnetism. CH4's actual tetrahedral geometry is distinguished from its flat sketch.
7. Water's δ+ / δ− are partial charges, not complete H+/O2−. Its bent arrangement gives net polarity; linear symmetric CO2 can have polar bonds but zero net molecular dipole.
8. Simple molecular solids differ from giant covalent networks (diamond/SiO2). Melting an ordinary molecular solid mainly overcomes intermolecular attraction, not necessarily its intramolecular covalent bonds. No “all covalent low-melting” rule.
9. **Not all ionic compounds dissolve in water; not all covalent compounds are insoluble or nonconducting.** AgCl/CaCO3 are sparingly soluble; sugar/ethanol can mix without significant ions; graphite conducts; HCl is covalent before water produces H3O+/Cl−. Dissolution, ion dissociation and molecular ionization are not synonyms. Solubility alone is insufficient for classification.
10. Solid ionic lattices have constrained ions; molten/dissolved mobile ions carry current. Metal conduction uses delocalized electrons, not solid cores flowing end-to-end. Conventional current is opposite electron drift. Both electrons and lattice vibrations can contribute to thermal conduction.
11. Both salt and sugar can crystallize; crystal appearance alone does not establish ionic bonding. Hypothetical property models make their candidate restrictions explicit and acknowledge exceptions and concentration/temperature/purity effects.
12. **Final diagram recount is authoritative:** the native ABD exercise shows A=H (1), **B=Na (2,8,1; Z11)**, D=Cl (2,8,7; Z17). B is neither O nor S. The XY exercise shows X=O (2,6), **Y=Mg (2,8,2; Z12)**, not Na. Adapted written work follows the actual dots and gives NaH/NaCl and MgO respectively. No absolute “sodium can never have covalent character” claim.
13. No unsafe home experiment: only teacher-controlled low-voltage observations supplied as educational data. No mains electricity in water, touching live circuits, tasting chemicals, smelling gases or independently heating unknowns.
